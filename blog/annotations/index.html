Why annotation deserves study

For the past few years, I have been studying one part of charts that is easy to take for granted: the annotations. These are the labels, arrows, shaded regions, reference lines, and color highlights that point at part of a chart and tell you why it matters. When they are done well, you barely notice them. You just get the point.

Look at Figure 1. Both versions show exactly the same data. On the left, you are on your own. On the right, a few gray bands, short notes, and arrows tell you where the peaks are and what happened. Amanda Cox, then a graphics editor at The New York Times, once said:

“The annotation layer is the most important thing we do.”

Amanda Cox, quoted by Ren et al.10

Research backs up the idea that this layer matters. The text in and around a chart changes what readers remember, what message they take away, and how much they trust it.12,13,18,14 Analysts use annotations to record their thinking, and teams use them to share it.1

And yet, when we read through 191 papers on annotation, most of them treated it as a side topic inside work about something else.1 So I made annotation the subject of my dissertation.8 This post is the short, readable version of what my collaborators and I found. Here is what is coming:

  • the seven types of annotation people actually use, and how often each one shows up;
  • why annotations usually work in small groups;
  • three questions that make it easier to design an annotation, or to critique one;
  • what professional newsrooms do with annotation;
  • how experienced practitioners make their decisions;
  • how our tools could treat annotations better;
  • five ways to check whether an annotation is actually right.

At the end, you will find a practical checklist you can use on your next chart. If you want the full methods and limitations, the papers are listed at the bottom.

What counts as an annotation

Whether something is an annotation depends on the job it does, not on what it looks like. A line can be data in one chart and a reference line in another. A color can stand for a category, or it can highlight a single bar. We call an element an annotation when it is added on top of the data to point something out, explain a relationship, add context, or guide the reader.

Annotation is also a set of decisions. Should I annotate this at all? What should I point out? How? The answers depend on the data, the evidence you have, your audience, where the chart will appear, and what you want it to say.

One warning before we go further: an annotation cannot rescue a chart that is built wrong for its job. In 2020, the Georgia Department of Public Health published a bar chart that sorted dates by case count instead of by time. Every number was correct, but the chart looked like a timeline, and it was not one.11 A note could have explained the order. If the point was change over time, though, the real fix was to put the dates back in order. That is why I think it is best to plan annotations together with the chart, not after it.

Seven types of annotation

To see what people really do, we went looking for annotated charts in the wild. We collected 8,768 images from Google Images with searches like “annotated scatterplot,” across 14 chart types. After removing duplicates and images that did not fit, two of us coded 2,677 images, and we analyzed the 1,888 annotated charts that the first coder identified. The two coders agreed closely (Cohen’s κ = 0.886).3

The same seven types kept appearing. Figure 2 squeezes all of them into one chart.

  • Enclosure. A full or partial boundary, such as a rectangle, ellipse, or bracket.
  • Connector. A line or arrow from an annotation to the thing it refers to.
  • Text. Words, numbers, or sentences about the data.
  • Glyph. A symbol or shape that marks a point or category.
  • Color. A change in color that highlights or separates elements.
  • Indicator. A mark for a trend, statistic, or threshold, like a trend line or an average line.
  • Geometric. A change to the chart’s shape, like pulling out a pie slice or zooming into one region.

Which types people used depended on the chart. Indicators were common in line charts, bar charts, histograms, and scatterplots, where there is an axis to draw a trend or threshold against. They were rare in pie charts and treemaps, which have no such axis. Geometric zooms show up in maps and pie charts, but zooming into a line chart can make readers lose the overall trend.

These numbers tell you what people make, not what works best for readers. They are still handy. If you are about to do something unusual for your chart type, it is good to know that you are breaking with common practice, so you can make sure it is on purpose.

Annotations work in groups

If there is one pattern that showed up in every one of our studies, it is this: annotations rarely work alone. We call a group of annotations that do one job together an ensemble. A label with an arrow pointing at a bar is the simplest one.

We first noticed this in a classroom study.2 Students in a visualization course got grouped bar charts and questions like “Which counties have fewer than 40 cases on May 1?” For each question, they listed the basic tasks needed to answer it (retrieve a value, filter, compute a derived value, find an extreme, sort). Then they annotated the chart to make the answer easier to find. We analyzed 20 of the 38 submissions. The rest had no annotations, or only minimal or unrelated ones.

The students used five types of annotation, which we split into 14 subtypes. Whenever one annotation was not enough, they combined two, three, or four. Sometimes the task was hard to show with one mark. Sometimes there was no room to put a label right next to the data. The pieces of an ensemble can lean on each other in two ways:

  • One-way. One piece works alone, and the other just helps. A bracket can mark a range by itself. The small “#” next to it, which signals a sum, cannot.
  • Two-way. Neither piece works alone. An arrow without text does not say what to sort, and the text without the arrow does not show which way.

Three things from this study are worth keeping in mind for your own charts:

  • Some tasks are harder to annotate. Pointing to a value or picking out a subset was easy, and students had many ways to do it. Sorting was the hardest, and they often fell back on writing it out in text. An arrow with text helped, but it gets messy when there are many items.
  • More pieces are not always better. Many of the three- and four-piece ensembles had parts that repeated each other. Two pieces would often have been enough.
  • The chart’s order changes the annotation. When dates were out of order, students needed more annotations (16 per chart on average, compared with 14 for charts in time order), because one box could no longer cover a date range.

The same groups showed up at large scale. In the 1,888 charts, the most common ensembles were enclosure + connector + text, connector + text, enclosure + text, and glyph + color.3 In the news graphics we looked at, combinations were more common than single annotations.4 So it helps to think about the whole group when you design or review annotations. Move a label closer, and its arrow may no longer be needed.

Three questions: why, how, and what

To make sense of all this, we borrowed Brehmer and Munzner’s task typology15 and split every annotation into three questions.3

Why?

The task it serves

  • Identify an element or region
  • Compare elements
  • Summarize a trend
  • Present added context

How?

The form it takes

  • One of the seven types
  • Or an ensemble of several

What?

The information it draws on

  • Internal: values from the data
  • Derived: computed from the data
  • External: from outside the data

When you are picking a form, the left side of Figure 5 is the part to look at. Here is what it shows:

If the task is to Types we saw doing it
Identify something All seven types
Compare elements Most types. Geometric annotations were used only to identify.
Summarize a trend Text, glyphs, and indicators
Present added context Text, often with a connector to show what it refers to

The third question is the easiest one to skip, so I want to spend a moment on it. A text label can show a number that is already in the data (internal). It can state an average or a trend you calculated (derived). Or it can explain an event that the data cannot show at all (external). All three look like text. But each one is backed by something different. An internal label is checked against the data. A derived one also depends on the calculation. An external one depends on a source outside the chart. In our corpus, internal annotations appeared in 973 charts, derived ones in 330, and external ones in 994.

Worked example

To try these questions out, we took an Economist line chart about “deaths of despair” in the United States, removed its annotations, and added them back one question at a time.3

  • Point out when OxyContin was introduced. Why: identify. How: a vertical indicator line, plus text explaining the event. What: external, from the article.
  • Show the earlier decline in deaths. Why: summarize. How: a trend indicator with text. What: the trend line is derived from the data; the explanation is external.
  • Compare two demographic groups. Why: compare. How: color and an enclosure to mark the gap, then a connector and text to say what the gap means. What: external.

Going one question at a time made every choice explicit. It also made it obvious what each annotation needed to be true.

The same three questions work well for critique. Pick any annotation in a finished chart and ask: what is it for, how is it built, and what backs up what it says?

Annotation in professional news graphics

Major newsrooms publish some of the most heavily annotated charts around, so we wanted to see what they do. We studied 72 static bar charts, line charts, and scatterplots from The New York Times, The Economist, The Wall Street Journal, and The Washington Post, published between 2017 and 2023. We read each chart together with its caption and its article.4 Five patterns kept coming up:

  • Groups, not singles. Combinations like connectors with text, or highlights with text, were more common than single annotations.
  • Highlights follow the story. Shaded regions and colored bars marked the parts of the chart that the article talked about.
  • The article moves into the chart. Short notes tied points from the article to specific values, periods, or events.
  • Caption and annotations share the work. The caption gave the main message. The annotations pinned it to specific places in the chart.
  • Key numbers get written out. Important values were often stated in text, even when the axis already showed them.

These patterns describe what professionals do. They were not tested on readers. Still, they suggest a good habit: think of the chart, its caption, and the text around it as one piece. Before you add an annotation, check whether the caption or the article already says it. Then ask whether it would still make sense if someone shared the chart on its own.

How practitioners decide

A finished chart shows what someone made, but not why. So we asked. We interviewed ten people who regularly make annotated charts in their work, including data analysts, a graphics editor, a visualization designer, a consultant, a professor, and managers. Most of them walked us through five to seven of their own charts. Then we asked seven visualization educators to react to what we found: where it holds, where it falls apart, and how they would teach it.5

Practitioners annotate for three main reasons. They want to point out what matters and give enough context. They want to set the order in which people read the chart. And they want the chart to make sense when it travels without its article. As one of them put it, “everyone screenshots things.”

  1. Start with the audience and the stakes

    Figure out who will read the chart and what they need from it. If the chart is likely to be shared on its own, or a misreading would be costly, put the key caveats right on the chart. Keep them short.

    Educators pointed out that every extra note adds clutter and has to be kept up to date, and that no design can fully stop someone who wants to misuse a chart.

  2. Put text next to the data first

    Place a label right beside what it describes whenever you can. If there is no room, use a short arrow or draw one box around a small group. Use a legend or numbered key only when direct labels would crowd the chart, and put the key in the same order as the chart. One practitioner said, “a legend in a figure is a lot more cognitive burden than a well placed annotation.”

    That does not mean label everything. As one educator said, label every mark and “I might as well just use a table.”

  3. Make one annotation the main one

    Pick the one annotation that matters most and give it the strongest spot and the most emphasis. Keep the others lighter. If a smaller note starts competing with it, cut it, merge it, or tone it down.

  4. Connect labels to data with more than color

    Coloring a label to match its line or bar helps, but only when the colors are easy to tell apart. Add a second cue too: put the label close, add a small arrow, a color swatch, or a box. And make sure colored text is still easy to read.

    Educators disagreed here. Some wanted label colors to match the data exactly. Others preferred a separate, consistent style for annotations.

  5. Decide whether annotations blend in or stand out

    Some practitioners style annotations like the rest of the chart, so everything reads as one piece. Others make them look different on purpose, so readers know they are added commentary. Both work. Choose based on what the annotation is for, and use the same style across related charts.

  6. Set an annotation budget

    Keep visible only what the main message needs at first glance. Move the rest to a tooltip, the caption, or the text. Some practitioners had a rule of thumb. One aimed for about three annotations and five at most. Others did the opposite: annotate everything, then delete what does not help.

    Educators pushed back on fixed numbers. One described the goal as “complexity communicated with clarity,” and said that might take one annotation, or a hundred.

These six ideas pull on each other. Add context for a broader audience, and the chart gets denser, so the main annotation needs to stand out more. Move a label, and its link to the data changes. I think of them as a shared vocabulary for thinking and talking about annotation, not as rules.

Static and interactive charts

Interactive charts can tuck details into tooltips and keep the view clean. But hover does not work on every device, and plenty of readers never hover at all. One educator said it simply: “If important context is only on hover, many users will never see it.” The practitioners we talked to kept the main message visible from the start and used tooltips only for extras.

How practitioners check their work

Most of the checking was quick and informal. A few habits came up again and again:

  • Show the chart to someone else and ask if the main point is clear without any explanation.
  • Stop revising once the chart answers the questions stakeholders are likely to ask.
  • Test it on people outside the field. One practitioner asked three people and treated three yeses as a sign the chart was ready.
  • Delete any annotation that does not change how the chart is read.

Educators added one thing: match the effort to the stakes. A quick peer review is fine for routine work. If a chart could steer an important decision, test it more carefully with real readers.

Finally, annotation is rarely taught on its own. None of the seven educators had a dedicated unit on it.

Annotation in our tools

Nine of the ten practitioners built their chart in one tool and then finished the annotations in another, such as PowerPoint, Illustrator, or Figma. That gives them precise control over placement and type. The catch is that the annotations are no longer connected to the data. Change the data or the layout, and the annotations have to be redone by hand. As one practitioner said, “labels need to move with the data.”

Code-based tools have their own version of this problem. Vega-Lite has no annotation building block, so you build annotations out of text and line marks in extra layers. ggplot2 and Observable Plot give you text and shape layers, but nothing that records which point a note belongs to.1 The annotation ends up scattered across mark definitions, coordinates, and layout code.

That is the problem AnnoGram tries to solve.6 It extends the Grammar of Graphics so that annotations are a proper part of the chart specification. Every annotation has four parts:

  • Target: what it refers to. This can be data points, an axis, a part of the chart such as the title or legend, or a fixed position.
  • Type: what kind of annotation it is, such as text, an enclosure, a connector, or an indicator.
  • Placement: where it goes relative to its target. AnnoGram picks a default, and you can override it.
  • Style: how it looks.

Here is a simplified example, adapted from the dissertation:

{
  "target": { "type": "data-expr", "expr": "datum.date == '2021-01-11'" },
  "text": { "text": "The previous case peak was ...", "position": { ... } },
  "connector": { "curve": "natural" }
}

Because the annotation points to its target and not to fixed pixel positions, it can survive changes. In Figure 8, the same annotation specification is reused on three different charts of the same dataset. AnnoGram finds the target in each chart and places the annotation, with no rewriting.

When we compared it with eight existing tools, having built-in annotation constructs cut down the work of assembling annotations by hand. AnnoGram is still a proof of concept built on Vega-Lite. It does not cover every chart layout or annotation type yet. And it does not decide what to annotate. That part is still up to you.

How to tell whether an annotation is right

More and more annotations are now made by tools, templates, and AI models. That makes an old question more pressing: how do you know an annotation is correct? A label can look polished and still point at the wrong bar.

We came up with five criteria. Each one catches a different kind of mistake.8,7 Figure 9 shows one failure of each, for the task “Label the smallest bar with ‘Smallest (value)’.”

Criterion The question to ask
Target correctness Does it clearly point to the right mark, series, or region?
Task accuracy Is what it says relevant, and backed by the data or the context you were given?
Spatial feasibility Can you read it and tell what it belongs to, without it hiding the data?
Chart preservation Are the data, scales, marks, and encodings exactly as they were before?
Stylistic coherence Does it fit the chart’s fonts, colors, and hierarchy?

Why five separate checks? Because each one can fail while the others pass. A correct number can sit on the wrong bar. A neatly placed label can say something the data does not support. A tool can add exactly the right label and quietly change a bar’s height along the way. If you only ask “does this look good?”, you will miss these.

The criteria also tell you what to recheck when a chart changes. These are rough pairings, not hard rules:

  • After filtering or reordering, check that every annotation still points to the right thing.
  • After a data update, check that stated values and calculated claims are still true.
  • After resizing, check that labels are readable, do not overlap, and still fit the style.
  • After editing the chart to add annotations, check that the data and encodings did not change.

We put these criteria to work in AnnoBench, a benchmark for annotation generation led by my colleague Md Rahat-uz-Zaman.7 It pairs about 342 charts, from professional news graphics and the Vega and Vega-Lite galleries, with more than 650 annotation tasks. It changes how the chart is given to a model (as an image, SVG, a grammar specification, or code), how much description comes with it, and how specific the instructions are. When we tested current language and vision-language models, we found:

  • Models did best when they got the chart as code. Given an image, they often distorted the chart itself while adding the annotation.
  • Specific instructions worked better than open-ended goals. Given only a goal, models mostly used plain text, arrows, and lines, and rarely drew enclosures.
  • Richer chart descriptions helped a bit with picking the right target and saying relevant things, but did nothing for keeping the chart intact.
  • Models used as automatic judges were too generous with image outputs that looked fine but had changed the underlying chart.

So today’s models are better at describing a chart than at editing it faithfully. If you use them to annotate, the checklist below has a few suggestions.

What we still do not know

Most of this work describes charts and what practitioners told us. We do not yet know which annotation choices help readers most. We also need to learn how annotations should behave in interactive and responsive charts, how people author them over time, how consistently different people can apply the five checks, and how people and AI tools should split the work. If you are working on any of these, I would love to hear from you.
