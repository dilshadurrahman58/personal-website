Why annotation deserves study

A chart can show the data accurately and still leave the reader unsure what to look at. Annotations help with that. They are the labels, callouts, arrows, shaded regions, reference lines, and color highlights that point to part of a chart and say something about it.

In Figure 1, both versions contain the same data. Only the right one tells you which periods matter and why. Amanda Cox, then a graphics editor at The New York Times, put the point this way:

“The annotation layer is the most important thing we do.”

Amanda Cox, quoted by Ren et al.10

Research supports this. Text in and around charts affects what readers remember, what message they take away, and how much they trust it.12,13,18,14 Annotations also help analysts record their reasoning and help teams share interpretations.1

Even so, when we surveyed the field, we found that knowledge about annotation was scattered. Across 191 papers, annotation was usually a side topic inside work about something else, such as storytelling, collaboration, or exploratory analysis.1 My dissertation tries to bring this knowledge together.8 This post walks through its main ideas. The papers listed at the end have the methods, the details, and the limits.

What counts as an annotation

An annotation is defined by its role, not by its shape. The same line can be data in one chart and a reference line in another. The same color can encode a category or highlight one bar. So we treat an element as an annotation when it adds to the chart’s data encoding to direct attention, explain a relationship, add context, or guide interpretation.

Annotation is also a process. Someone has to decide whether to annotate, what to annotate, and how. Those decisions depend on the data, the evidence available, the audience, the medium, and the purpose of the chart.

One limit is worth stating early. An annotation cannot fix a chart whose encoding does not fit the task. In 2020, a Georgia Department of Public Health bar chart ordered dates by case count instead of by time. The values were correct, but the chart invited a reading over time that the order did not support.11 A note could have explained the ordering. If the goal was to show change over time, putting the dates back in order was the real fix. Annotation should be designed together with the encoding and layout, not added afterward to patch them.

Seven types of annotation

To see what annotation looks like in practice, we collected 8,768 images from Google Images using searches like “annotated scatterplot” across 14 chart types. After removing duplicates and images that did not fit the study, two coders classified 2,677 images. We analyzed the 1,888 annotated charts identified by the first coder. Agreement between the two coders was high (Cohen’s κ = 0.886).3

We found seven recurring types. Figure 2 shows all seven in one chart.

  • Enclosure. A full or partial boundary, such as a rectangle, ellipse, or bracket.
  • Connector. A line or arrow that links an annotation to what it refers to.
  • Text. Words, values, or sentences about the data.
  • Glyph. A symbol or shape that marks a point or category.
  • Color. A change in hue, lightness, or another color property that highlights or separates elements.
  • Indicator. A mark for a trend, statistic, or threshold, such as a trend line or an average line.
  • Geometric. A change to the chart’s geometry, such as enlarging a pie slice or zooming into a region.

The mix of types changed with the chart type. Indicators were common in line charts, bar charts, histograms, and scatterplots, where there is an axis to mark a trend or threshold against. They were rare in pie charts and treemaps, which have no such axis. These counts describe what people make. They do not tell us which choices work best for readers.

Annotations work in groups

One of the clearest patterns across our studies is that annotations rarely work alone. We call a group of annotations that work together an ensemble. A connector with a text label is the simplest example.

We first saw this in a classroom study.2 Students in a visualization course received grouped bar charts and questions such as “Which counties have fewer than 40 cases on May 1?” For each question, they named the low-level analytic tasks it required (retrieve value, filter, compute derived value, find extremum, sort) and then annotated the chart to make the question easier to answer. We analyzed 20 of the 38 submissions. The others had no annotations, or only minimal or unrelated ones.

Students used five types of annotation, which we divided into 14 subtypes. They combined two, three, or four types when one was not enough, either because the task was hard to show or because there was no room to place a label directly. Inside an ensemble, the parts can depend on each other in two ways:

  • One-way. One part works alone and the other only adds to it. A bracket can mark a range by itself. A small “#” next to it, signaling a sum, cannot.
  • Two-way. Neither part works alone. An arrow without text does not say what to sort, and the text without the arrow does not show the direction.

Some tasks were easier to annotate than others. Retrieving a value and filtering had many options. Sorting was the hardest, and students often fell back on text. Charts with dates out of order needed more annotations (16 per chart on average, versus 14 for charts in time order), because a date range could no longer be marked with one enclosure.

The pattern held at larger scale. In the 1,888-chart corpus, the most common ensembles were enclosure + connector + text, connector + text, enclosure + text, and glyph + color.3 In professional news graphics, ensembles were more common than single annotations.4 The practical point: when you design, review, or build tools for annotation, the unit is often the group, not the single element.

Three questions: why, how, and what

To organize these findings, we adapted Brehmer and Munzner’s task typology.15 It separates three questions about any annotation.3

Why?

The task it serves

  • Identify an element or region
  • Compare elements
  • Summarize a trend
  • Present added context

How?

The form it takes

  • One of the seven types
  • Or an ensemble of several

What?

The information it draws on

  • Internal: values from the data
  • Derived: computed from the data
  • External: from outside the data

In the corpus, every type could support identifying, and most could support comparing. Text was the only type that presented added context on its own.

The third question is easy to overlook, and it matters. A text label can show a value already in the data (internal), state an average or a trend computed from the data (derived), or explain an event that the data cannot show (external). It looks the same in all three cases, but each one needs different evidence to be correct. In the corpus, internal annotations appeared in 973 charts, derived ones in 330, and external ones in 994.

These three questions are useful for critique as well as design. For any annotation, you can ask what task it serves, how it is built, and what supports what it says.

Annotation in professional news graphics

News graphics are a setting where annotation is especially visible. We studied 72 static bar charts, line charts, and scatterplots from The New York Times, The Economist, The Wall Street Journal, and The Washington Post, published between 2017 and 2023, and read each one together with its caption and article.4 Five patterns stood out:

  • Ensembles were the norm. Combinations such as connectors with text, or highlights with text, were more common than single annotations.
  • Highlighting followed the story. Shaded regions and colored bars marked the parts of the chart that the article discussed.
  • Article content moved into the chart. Short descriptions tied points from the article to specific values, periods, or events.
  • Captions and annotations split the work. The caption gave the main message, and the annotations pinned that message to places in the chart.
  • Key numbers were written out. Values were often stated in text even when an axis already showed them.

The lesson is that an annotated chart is often one part of a larger piece. When you judge how much annotation a chart needs, it helps to consider what the caption and surrounding text already carry.

How practitioners decide

Finished charts show what people made, not why. To learn the reasoning, we interviewed ten practitioners who regularly make annotated charts, including data analysts, a graphics editor, a visualization designer, a consultant, a professor, and managers. They typically walked us through five to seven of their own charts. We then asked seven visualization educators to reflect on what we found: where it holds, where it breaks, and how they would teach it.5

Practitioners annotate for three main reasons: to point out what matters and give enough context, to set a reading order, and to keep a chart understandable when it travels without its article. As one practitioner put it, “everyone screenshots things.”

  1. Start from the audience and the stakes

    Decide who will read the chart and what they need to get from it. If the chart is likely to be shared on its own, or a misreading would be costly, put essential caveats on the chart itself. Keep them short.

    Educators warned that extra notes add clutter and must be maintained, and that no design can fully prevent deliberate misuse.

  2. Put text next to the data first

    Place a label beside what it describes when you can. If there is no room, use a short connector or a single enclosure. Use a legend or numbered key only when direct labels would crowd the chart. One practitioner said, “a legend in a figure is a lot more cognitive burden than a well placed annotation.”

    Not everything needs a label. As one educator noted, label everything and “I might as well just use a table.”

  3. Make one annotation the main one

    Choose one primary annotation and give it the strongest position and emphasis. Keep the rest visibly secondary. If a secondary note competes with it, remove it, merge it, or tone it down.

  4. Link labels to data with more than color

    Matching a label’s color to its line or bar helps, but only when the colors are easy to tell apart. Pair color with proximity, a small connector, a swatch, or an enclosure, and keep text readable.

    Educators differed on whether label colors should match the data exactly or follow a separate annotation style.

  5. Decide whether annotations blend in or stand apart

    Some practitioners style annotations like the rest of the chart, so it reads as one piece. Others make them visibly different, so readers know they are added commentary. Either can work. Pick based on the role, and apply the style consistently.

  6. Set an annotation budget for the medium

    Keep visible only what the main message needs at first glance. Move details to a tooltip or the surrounding text. Some practitioners used rules of thumb. One aimed for about three annotations and at most five.

    Educators rejected fixed numbers. One described the goal as “complexity communicated with clarity,” which might take one annotation or a hundred.

These considerations are connected. Adding context for a wider audience adds density, which makes hierarchy more important. Moving a label changes how it links to its target. We offer them as a shared vocabulary for reasoning and critique, not as rules.

Two more findings stood out. First, interactive charts can move detail into tooltips, but hover is not always available, so the core message should stay visible. One educator said, “If important context is only on hover, many users will never see it.” Second, annotation is rarely taught directly. None of the seven educators had a dedicated unit on it.

Annotation in our tools

Nine of the ten practitioners built their chart in one tool and finished the annotations in another, such as PowerPoint, Illustrator, or Figma. This gives precise control over placement and type. It also breaks the link between the annotation and the data, so any change to the data or layout means redoing the annotations by hand. As one practitioner put it, “labels need to move with the data.”

Code-based tools have a related problem. Vega-Lite has no annotation primitive, so authors build annotations from text and rule marks in extra layers. ggplot2 and Observable Plot offer text and shape layers, but nothing that says “this note refers to that point.”1 The meaning of the annotation ends up spread across mark definitions, coordinates, and layout code.

AnnoGram is our attempt to fix this.6 It extends the Grammar of Graphics so that annotation is a first-class part of a chart specification. Each annotation has four parts:

  • Target: what it refers to (data points, an axis, a chart part such as the title or legend, or a fixed position).
  • Type: the kind of annotation, such as text, an enclosure, a connector, or an indicator.
  • Placement: relative to the target, with sensible defaults that the author can override.
  • Style: how it looks.

A simplified example, adapted from the dissertation:

{
  "target": { "type": "data-expr", "expr": "datum.date == '2021-01-11'" },
  "text": { "text": "The previous case peak was ...", "position": { ... } },
  "connector": { "curve": "natural" }
}

Because the annotation points to its target rather than to fixed pixels, it can survive changes. In Figure 6, one annotation specification is reused across three charts of the same dataset. AnnoGram finds the target in each chart and places the annotation without any rewriting.

In a comparison with eight existing tools, built-in annotation constructs reduced the need to assemble annotations by hand. AnnoGram is a proof of concept built on Vega-Lite, and it does not yet cover every chart composition or annotation type. It also does not decide what to annotate. That judgment stays with the author.

How to tell whether an annotation is right

Tools, templates, and AI models increasingly produce annotations. That raises a simple question: how do we know a produced annotation is correct? A polished label can still point at the wrong bar.

We propose five criteria, each covering a different way an annotation can fail.8,7 Figure 7 shows one failure of each, for the task “Label the smallest bar with ‘Smallest (value)’.”

Criterion The question it asks
Target correctness Does it point clearly to the intended mark, series, or region?
Task accuracy Is what it says relevant to the task and supported by the data or the given context?
Spatial feasibility Is it readable and clearly linked to its target without hiding evidence?
Chart preservation Are the data, scales, marks, and encodings unchanged after it was added?
Stylistic coherence Does it fit the chart’s typography, color, and hierarchy?

Keeping these separate matters because they fail independently. A correct value can sit on the wrong bar. A well-placed label can make an unsupported claim. A tool can add the right label while quietly changing a bar height. A single score of “good” or “bad” would hide which part failed.

We applied these criteria in AnnoBench, a benchmark for annotation generation first-authored by Md Rahat-uz-Zaman.7 It pairs about 342 charts, from professional news graphics and the Vega and Vega-Lite galleries, with more than 650 annotation tasks. It varies how the chart is given to a model (image, SVG, grammar, or code), how much description comes with it, and how specific the instructions are. A few findings from testing current language and vision-language models:

  • Models did best when given the chart as code. With images, they often distorted the chart itself while adding the annotation.
  • Explicit instructions worked better than open-ended goals. Given only a goal, models tended to fall back on simple text, arrows, and lines, and rarely chose enclosures.
  • Richer chart descriptions helped somewhat with targeting and relevance, but not with preserving the chart.
  • Models used as automatic judges tended to over-credit image outputs that looked plausible but had changed the underlying chart.

In short, these models are better at saying what a chart shows than at editing the chart faithfully to show it. Evaluation should check each criterion, not just whether the result looks convincing.

What to take away

Across these studies, annotation looks like a design space with real structure: recurring types, recurring groups, and a few clear dimensions. Yet the right choice still depends on the chart, the evidence, the audience, the medium, and the purpose.

If you make charts

  • Design annotations together with the encoding and layout. They cannot fix a mismatched chart.
  • For each annotation, know its task, its target, and its source: internal, derived, or external. Each needs different evidence.
  • Think in groups. A label, a connector, and a highlight often do one job together.
  • Place text near the data, choose one main annotation, and set a budget for the medium.
  • Plan for the screenshot. Decide which context must stay attached to the chart.
  • When you review a chart, check the five criteria one at a time instead of asking whether it looks right.

If you study or build visualization tools

  • Represent annotations as linked objects with targets, not as loose text boxes on top of a chart.
  • Keep track of what each annotation depends on, so tools can flag notes that a data update has made wrong.
  • Evaluate generated annotations criterion by criterion. Visual plausibility is not correctness.

Open questions

Most of this work describes charts and reported practice. It does not yet show which annotation choices help readers most. Other open questions include how annotations should behave in interactive and responsive charts, how people actually author them over time, how well the five criteria can be applied consistently by different evaluators, and how people and AI systems should share annotation work.
